Economists use network centrality to measure returns to network position and to target interventions. Both require costly full-network data, but a simple random sample yields biased estimates. I propose a sequential sampling design that interviews a random household from the household roster, records its links, and moves to a random contact. Instead of modeling network formation, I condition on the network. For a fixed sampling fraction, the estimated eigenvector centrality is consistent at the rate of the average degree over the largest eigenvalue, under degree heterogeneity and nonvanishing spectral gaps. Under the null of a zero slope, the slope on estimated centrality is exactly unbiased and its $t$-statistic is asymptotically equivalent to the full-network statistic. In the villages of Banerjee et al. (2019), sequentially surveying half the households reproduces the full-network slopes within about one percent, whereas a simple random sample understates them by eleven to twelve percent.
