Economists use network centrality to measure the returns to an individual's network position and to target interventions such as information diffusion and technology adoption. Both require the full network, but a simple random sample yields biased estimates. We propose a sequential sampling design: interview a household, record its links in full, and move to a randomly chosen contact. Instead of modeling network formation, we condition on the network, and sampling generates the only source of randomness. For a fixed sampling fraction, the estimator is consistent at the rate of average degree over largest eigenvalue, provided this ratio is of smaller order than the inverse logarithm of the population size and both the adjacency matrix and its row-normalized counterpart have nonvanishing spectral gaps. Under the null of a zero slope, the regression slope on estimated centrality is exactly unbiased and its $t$-statistic is asymptotically equivalent to the full-network statistic. In the Indian village networks of \citet{banerjee2019}, a sequential survey of half the households recovers the full-network regression slopes to within one percent, whereas a simple random sample of the same size understates them by eleven to twelve percent.
